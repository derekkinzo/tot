# Evidence Quality

Reference material for assessing how strongly a piece of evidence supports or
refutes a hypothesis. Sources are primary academic works in philosophy of
science and intelligence analysis, with standard tertiary sources for
widely-known terminology.

## 1. Mill's Methods of Experimental Inquiry (Mill, 1843)

From *A System of Logic, Ratiocinative and Inductive*, Book III, Chapter VIII.
Five canons for inferring causal connection from observed regularities.

### Method of Agreement
- **Definition**: If two or more instances of a phenomenon share only one
  antecedent circumstance in common, that circumstance is the cause (or effect).
- **Applies when**: Multiple positive cases of the effect are available.
- **Strength**: Weak. Cannot rule out unobserved common factors. Suggests
  correlation, not causation.
- **Example**: Three patients with the same condition all consumed water from the same well; or three failed runs all used the same component version.

### Method of Difference
- **Definition**: If an instance where the phenomenon occurs and an instance
  where it does not occur differ in only one circumstance, that circumstance
  is the cause (or effect, or indispensable part of the cause).
- **Applies when**: A controlled comparison between presence and absence is
  possible.
- **Strength**: Strong. Closest of Mill's methods to a controlled experiment.
- **Example**: Two trials identical in every respect except one variable diverge in outcome — that variable is the cause of the divergence.

### Joint Method of Agreement and Difference
- **Definition**: If two or more instances in which the phenomenon occurs
  share a circumstance, while two or more instances in which it does not
  occur lack that circumstance, the circumstance is the cause (or effect).
- **Applies when**: Both positive and negative cases are observable.
- **Strength**: Stronger than Agreement alone, weaker than Difference under
  controlled conditions.
- **Example**: All affected cases share an exposure that all unaffected cases lack.

### Method of Residues
- **Definition**: Subtract from a phenomenon the part known by prior induction
  to be the effect of certain antecedents; the residue is the effect of the
  remaining antecedents.
- **Applies when**: Some causal contributions are already established.
- **Strength**: Conditional on the prior inductions being correct.
- **Example**: An observed rise of 200 units; 150 are explained by a known prior contributor; the residual 50 must be explained by something else.

### Method of Concomitant Variation
- **Definition**: Whatever phenomenon varies in any manner whenever another
  phenomenon varies in some particular manner is either a cause, an effect,
  or connected through some fact of causation.
- **Applies when**: Variables are continuous and co-vary.
- **Strength**: Useful when removal is impossible. Vulnerable to confounding.
- **Example**: The magnitude of the effect rises monotonically with the level of the candidate cause.

## 2. Popper Falsificationism (Popper, 1959)

From *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* (English ed., orig. *Logik der
Forschung*, 1934).

- **Severity of test (riskiness)**: A test is severe to the degree that the
  hypothesis forbade the observed outcome. Predictions that could easily fail
  but did not provide stronger corroboration than predictions that were almost
  certain to succeed (Popper 1959, §82–§83).
- **Asymmetry of refutation**: Universal statements cannot be verified by any
  finite number of confirming instances but can be refuted by a single
  counter-instance. The valid form is *modus tollens* (¬Q, P→Q ⊢ ¬P).
  Confirming the consequent (Q, P→Q ⊢ P) is a formal fallacy and does not
  establish P.
- **Ad hoc rescue warning**: When a falsifying observation appears, do not
  introduce auxiliary hypotheses whose sole purpose is to preserve the
  original claim. Popper called such moves "conventionalist twists" and
  excluded them from scientific practice (Popper 1959, §19–§20). The
  historical analogue is adding epicycles to save a geocentric model.

## 3. Heuer ACH Diagnosticity Matrix (Heuer, 1999)

From *Psychology of Intelligence Analysis*, Chapter 8, "Analysis of Competing
Hypotheses". Published by the Center for the Study of Intelligence, CIA.

- **Matrix structure**: Hypotheses across the top, evidence down the side.
  Each cell records whether the evidence is consistent (C), inconsistent (I),
  or not applicable (N/A) with that hypothesis.
- **Diagnostic evidence**: Evidence that is consistent with one hypothesis
  and inconsistent with others. Only diagnostic evidence discriminates.
- **Non-diagnostic evidence**: Evidence consistent with multiple hypotheses
  carries no discriminating weight, regardless of how confidently it is
  established.
- **Inconsistency-counting over consistency-counting**: Heuer argues the
  analyst should focus on which hypotheses the evidence *refutes*, not which
  it *fits*. The hypothesis with the fewest inconsistencies is preferred,
  not the one with the most consistencies. The preference holds because the
  matrix scores one evidence set against every hypothesis; a hypothesis no
  observation was checked against scores zero inconsistencies without having
  been tested at all, and is not thereby preferred.

## 4. Evidence Hierarchy (singular causation)

Ordering for evidence about a particular event or token causal claim, from
strongest to weakest:

1. **Direct observation** of the mechanism in operation.
2. **Controlled experiment / intervention** (manipulation of the candidate
   cause while holding other factors fixed).
3. **Controlled comparison** (natural experiment, matched cases).
4. **Correlational data** (co-occurrence without manipulation).
5. **Inference from prior beliefs** (deduction from accepted background
   theory).
6. **Analogy** to a structurally similar case.

This hierarchy applies to *singular* causation — claims about what caused a
specific token event. Universal claims (lawlike generalisations) fall under
Popper's framework: they cannot be verified by accumulated singular
observations and must be assessed by their falsifiability and the severity
of the tests they have survived.

## Practical Checks

- **Directness**: did you observe the mechanism, or infer it?
- **Independence**: do sources share a common cause?
- **Diagnosticity**: does this evidence rule out alternatives?
- **Completeness**: have you sought refutation?
